Put the learner in the driver's seat.

What changes when the learner motivates, drives and pays attention, and the adult's job becomes getting the environment right. For anyone who helps people learn: facilitators, educators, experts and parents.

The comfort of structure is real. You know what will be covered, the room looks orderly, and nobody can say you didn't teach it. Most of us were taught this way, and it's how most educators are trained.

The research doesn't say throw structure away. Teachers who gave the clearest structure were also the ones who gave the most choice, and both kept students engaged. The opposite of structure is chaos, not freedom. What changes is where the structure lives: it moves out of the lesson plan and into the room.

Jang, Reeve & Deci 2010 · Reeve & Cheon 2021

2What the driver's seat means

The learner does three things. Our job is to make the environment right for them.

The learner motivates

The reason to learn is the learner's own. They want to find something out, not finish a task for someone else.

The learner drives

The learner makes the real choices: what to explore, where to start, when to change direction.

The learner pays attention

The learner decides where to look and how long to stay. Attention follows what they chose.

What we put in the room

Materials

Real, open-ended and enough to go round. Things with more than one answer inside them.

Good sign: learners use materials in ways you didn't plan.

A documentation zine

Where the thinking is held: what I noticed, what I plan, what I wonder, what I found. It frees the head and shows the learning.

Good sign: pages full of sketches and questions, not neat answers.

An inspiration area

Examples, opened-up objects, books, other people's work. A place to get unstuck without being told what to do.

Good sign: learners walk over when stuck, then walk back with an idea.

Freedom

Choice of what, how, with whom, and where to move.

Good sign: different learners are doing different things at the same time.

A clear frame

The time, the safety rules, the room agreements, and how to get help. Freedom works because these are fixed.

Good sign: nobody asks "what are we supposed to do?"

3What choosing does to a learner

When a learner makes a real choice, something shifts inside them. It tends to go like this.

  1. "This is mine."Choosing creates ownership. The work stops being an assignment.
  2. "I care how it turns out."The reason to keep going moves inside the learner.
  3. "I'm looking closely."Attention narrows on the thing they picked.
  4. "I'll keep trying."When it gets hard, they stay, because the goal is theirs.
  5. "I did that."Pride, and the belief that they can do it again.

Offering choice is one of seven autonomy-supportive behaviours. Across 51 programmes, 38 of them randomised trials, students of teachers trained in them felt more autonomous, were more motivated and engaged, and felt less frustrated.

Reeve & Cheon 2021

When choice backfires

Too many options

Learners freeze. Offer three to five real starting points, not the whole world.

A choice that doesn't matter

Picking a colour isn't ownership. The choice has to change what they learn or how.

Choice with no support

Freedom without a frame feels like being left alone. Unguided discovery does worse than plain teaching.

Research on choice also suggests a few real options work better than many (Patall, Cooper and Robinson 2008, not yet reopened). Alfieri et al. 2011

4When it gets hard

Frustration and distraction aren't signs the approach failed. They're part of it. Here's what to look for and what to do.

Frustration

You see: Sighing, pushing it away, "this is stupid", sometimes tears.

What's going on: The gap feels too big, or they feel watched and judged.

Try: Name it: "This bit is really fiddly." Then make the next step smaller, or climb one rung of the hint ladder.

Avoid: Take it out of their hands and fix it.

Distraction

You see: Wandering, chatting off-topic, fiddling, reaching for a phone.

What's going on: They've lost the thread, the task is too easy or too hard, their head is full, or they just need a break.

Try: Walk over and ask "what are you working on?" Offer a challenge card or a trip to the inspiration area. A short break is fine.

Avoid: Call them out in front of everyone.

Can't choose

You see: Hovering, "I don't know", waiting to be told.

What's going on: Too many options, or worry about choosing wrong.

Try: Narrow it to two. Ask "which would you open if nobody was watching?" Let them watch a friend for a few minutes first.

Avoid: Choose for them straight away.

"I'm done" in five minutes

You see: Finished, bored, looking for the next thing.

What's going on: The floor was low and they haven't found the ceiling yet.

Try: "Show me. What surprised you? What would make it harder?" Hand over a challenge card.

Avoid: Give them an unrelated task to fill time.

Wants the answer

You see: Asking you, a friend or an AI to just tell them.

What's going on: They want relief from not knowing, or they've learnt that answers are the point.

Try: Ask "what have you tried?" Give a hint, not the answer. AI comes after an attempt, for hints only.

Avoid: Hand over the answer, or ban asking altogether.

Takes over the group

You see: One voice, one pair of hands, a partner who goes quiet.

What's going on: Confidence, excitement, or a habit of leading.

Try: Give roles: one opens, one records. Ask the quieter partner first.

Avoid: Let it run, or tell them off publicly.

Gives up quietly

You see: Sitting still, hiding their work, "it's fine".

What's going on: They've decided they're not good at this.

Try: Sit next to them. Start with what they've noticed. Make the next step tiny and winnable.

Avoid: Say "well done" for nothing, or leave them to it.

The hint ladder. Give the smallest help that gets someone moving, then step back: wait, ask what they've tried, nudge with a question or a curiosity card, show one step, then hand it back.

Alfieri et al. 2011 · Reeve & Cheon 2021 · Bastani et al. 2025

5The facilitator: where to put your attention

You're not the centre of the room any more. You're its most careful observer.

The whole room, every few minutes

Scan for three things: who's absorbed (leave them alone), who's stuck, who's drifting.

Feelings before tasks

A frustrated learner can't hear a hint. Name the feeling first.

The quiet ones

Silence can mean deep focus or quiet giving up. Go and look.

Signs of learning

Setting their own goal, moving from guessing to testing, explaining why, using materials in new ways, helping others.

Yourself

Notice your own urge to explain, rescue or tidy up. That urge is the signal to wait.

Exploratorium Learning Dimensions 2017

Communication cues

SayInstead of
"What have you noticed?""Let me show you."
"What have you tried?""No, that's wrong."
"You might try…""You need to…"
"What makes you say that?""Good job!"
"Being stuck is part of it.""It's easy, look."
"Tell me about this part.""What is it?"
"What do you want to find out next?""Are you done?"

With your body

  • Crouch or sit at the learner's level.
  • Keep your hands off their work. Behind your back helps.
  • After a question, wait at least ten seconds.
  • Move around the room. Don't stand guard at the front.
  • Carry a notebook and write down what you notice.

Reeve & Cheon 2021 · Project Zero thinking routines

6Noise, movement and a new kind of discipline

Old discipline

Quiet, still, in your seat, doing what you're told.

Refract discipline

Caring for your work, other people's work, the materials and the space.

A noisy room isn't a bad room. One question: is the noise about the work?

Noise that's fine

  • Talking about the work
  • Testing out loud
  • Laughing at a surprise
  • Explaining to a friend
  • Moving to see someone else's idea

Noise to check on

  • The same few voices for a long time
  • Off-topic for more than a few minutes
  • Someone is upset
  • Tools or materials used unsafely

Movement is part of thinking. Walking to the inspiration area, visiting another table to see how they did it, standing up to test something: movement carries ideas around the room. A learner who's moving is often a learner who's thinking. Thinking happens across people, tools and the room, not only in one head.

Agency by Design, Project Zero · Exploratorium Learning Dimensions 2017

7One approach, many formats

Taking things apart is one way in. The same approach works for any format where the learner can choose and find out.

FormatWhat it isAn open questionWhat the learner chooses
Take it apartAn object table. Learners choose one and open it up.What's actually going on inside this thing?Which object, which part first, which tool.
Investigation stationsFour or five stations around one big question. Learners move freely between them.What is sound?Which stations, in what order, how long to stay.
Make it workA challenge with materials and no instructions.How can one turn make two things move?What to build, how, and when it's good enough.
A mysterySomething puzzling to explain, with clues to investigate.Why did my dough rise and theirs didn't?Which clue to follow, which test to run.
A walkGo out and collect: sounds, smells, shapes, stories.What can I hear that I never noticed?Where to go, what to collect, how to record it.

Example: What is sound? in stations

One big question, four stations, free movement. Learners visit at least two and choose one for a deep dive. Each station has a question card, not instructions, and a zine page: I noticed, I think, I wonder.

Feel it

Hands on a speaker, rice on a drum, a balloon against your throat.

Question card: What's moving when there's a sound?

Change it

Rubber bands of different thickness on a box, bottles with different amounts of water.

Question card: What makes a sound higher or lower?

Send it

String telephones, long tubes, a wall to knock on.

Question card: Can sound travel through a string? Through a wall?

See it

A phone app that shows sound waves, a tuning fork in water.

Question card: What does a sound look like?

Inspiration area: an old speaker opened up, pictures of instruments from around the world, a list of strange sounds to explain. The session ends with "Wait, now I want to find out…", the same as every Refract workshop.

8How we want them to feel

A journey map for the learner and the facilitator, across one session. The dip in the middle is expected. Our job is to make sure it's held.

A design picture of how we hope a session feels, not measured data.

1. Arrive

Learner

Does: Walks in, looks around, sees materials out.

Often feels: Unsure, a bit curious

We want them to feel: Welcome, and curious

Opportunity: Materials already out invite a first touch.

Facilitator

Does: Greets each learner, points to the frame.

Often feels: Nervous about how it will go

Attention on: First impressions: who looks keen, who looks wary

Opportunity: Say early that getting stuck is expected.

2. Notice and choose

Learner

Does: Browses, handles things, picks one.

Often feels: Curious, sometimes torn

We want them to feel: "I want to try that one"

Opportunity: Real choice from the first minute creates ownership.

Facilitator

Does: Stays back, answers questions with questions.

Often feels: The urge to explain

Attention on: Who can't choose

Opportunity: Say less. Let the object raise the questions.

3. Plan a way in

Learner

Does: Says what they want to find out and how they'll start.

Often feels: Excited, a little unsure

We want them to feel: Purposeful

Opportunity: An "I want to find out…" line becomes their own goal.

Facilitator

Does: Asks "where will you start?"

Often feels: Steadier

Attention on: Plans that are too big or too vague

Opportunity: Help shrink a big plan to a first step.

4. Try and find out

Learner

Does: Opens, tests, sketches in the zine, moves around.

Often feels: Absorbed

We want them to feel: Absorbed

Opportunity: Following their own thread is where curiosity grows.

Facilitator

Does: Moves around, notices, writes things down.

Often feels: Uneasy about the noise and mess

Attention on: Absorbed (leave alone), stuck, drifting

Opportunity: Redefine discipline: is the noise about the work?

5. Get stuck

Learner

Does: Hits a wall: a screw won't turn, it won't make a sound.

Often feels: Frustrated

We want them to feel: "This is hard, and that's OK"

Opportunity: The struggle is where the learning happens, if support is there.

Facilitator

Does: Climbs the hint ladder, one rung at a time.

Often feels: Tense, the urge to rescue

Attention on: Feelings before tasks

Opportunity: Wait ten seconds. Ask what they've tried.

6. Check in on yourself

Learner

Does: Pauses, writes in the zine, maybe visits the inspiration area.

Often feels: Steadier

We want them to feel: Aware of their own progress

Opportunity: Noticing "I've already found three things" rebuilds confidence.

Facilitator

Does: Calls a short pause for everyone.

Often feels: Relieved

Attention on: Who needs a new way in

Opportunity: Ask "how's it going?" before "what's next?"

7. Make sense and share

Learner

Does: Shows what they found, or passes.

Often feels: Proud, sometimes shy

We want them to feel: "I did that"

Opportunity: Putting it in their own words makes it stick.

Facilitator

Does: Explains one idea using learners' discoveries.

Often feels: Rewarded

Attention on: Connecting what different learners found

Opportunity: Teach from their attempts, not from a script.

8. Leave

Learner

Does: Writes "Wait, now I want to find out…"

Often feels: Curious, wanting more

We want them to feel: Curious, and able to do it again

Opportunity: An open question to take home keeps learning going.

Facilitator

Does: Collects the zines and the last lines.

Often feels: Tired and full of stories

Attention on: What to change next time

Opportunity: Write a two-line pilot note while it's fresh.
