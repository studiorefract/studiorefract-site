Put the learner in the driver's seat.

How Studio Refract thinks about learning, for anyone who helps people learn: facilitators, educators, experts and parents.

Refract in one minute

  • The learner chooses the question. We design the frame around it.
  • A playful set-up draws people in before anyone explains anything.
  • Time is unhurried. Learners take as long as they need.
  • Getting stuck is expected. A steady facilitator gives the smallest help that works.
  • Mistakes are ladders. Each one is a step up, so adults meet them with curiosity, not correction.
  • Every session has five beats: notice and choose, plan a way in, try and find out, check in on yourself, make sense and share.
  • It ends with "Wait, now I want to find out…"

A

Why

What changes when the learner drives.

1Two ways to run a room

Both rooms have structure. In ours, it lives in the room, not in the lesson plan.

The adult drivesThe learner drives
What gets learntDecided by the adultChosen by the learner, inside a frame
Who holds attentionThe adult at the frontEach learner, on their own work
The adult's jobExplain, show, correctSet up, notice, ask, nudge
TimeFixed slots, move on at the bellUnhurried, stay while it's useful
A quiet room meansIt's going wellLook closer: absorbed, or stuck?
A mistake meansSomething to fixInformation to use
Success looks likeEveryone gets the same answerEveryone finds something out and wants more

Structure is comfortable. You know what gets covered, and it's how most of us were taught. The research doesn't say drop structure. Clear structure and real choice go together. The structure just moves out of the lesson plan and into the room.

Why it works: Autonomy with structure

2What the driver's seat means

The learner motivates, drives and pays attention. The adult makes that possible.

Motivates

Their own reason to find out, not a task for someone else.

Drives

They make the real choices: what, where to start, when to switch.

Pays attention

They decide where to look and how long to stay.

Why it works: Autonomy with structure

3What choosing does to a learner

A real choice makes the work theirs, and that keeps them going when it gets hard.

  1. "This is mine."
  2. "I care how it turns out."
  3. "I'm looking closely."
  4. "I'll keep trying."
  5. "I did that."

Offering real choice is one of seven teacher behaviours that, across 51 programmes, made students more motivated and engaged. Reeve & Cheon 2021

When choice backfires

Too many options

Learners freeze. Offer three to five.

A choice that doesn't matter

Picking a colour isn't ownership.

Choice with no support

Feels like being left alone, and teaches less.

B

Set up

What to put in place before anyone arrives.

4Eight things that make it work

Get these right and the learner can drive. They're all on the map.

  1. Materials. Real, open-ended, enough to go round. Good sign: Used in ways you didn't plan.
  2. A documentation zine. Holds the thinking: noticed, plan, wonder, found. Good sign: Sketches and questions, not neat answers.
  3. An inspiration area. Examples and opened-up objects for getting unstuck. Good sign: A stuck learner walks over and comes back with an idea.
  4. A playful set-up. The room invites play before anyone explains: a mystery box at the door, things half-hidden, a question on the wall. Good sign: They start touching and wondering before you say a word.
  5. Freedom to move. What, how, with whom, and where to go. Good sign: Different learners doing different things.
  6. A clear frame. Safety rules, room agreements, how to get help. Good sign: Nobody asks "what are we supposed to do?"
  7. Unhurried time. Long, unbroken time to look, try and try again. Plan fewer activities than you think. Good sign: Nobody gets pulled off something they're absorbed in.
  8. A strong facilitator. Steady, not in charge: holds the frame, reads the room, knows when to wait. Good sign: The room feels safe to be stuck in.

5Noise, movement and a new kind of discipline

Discipline here means care, not quiet. Ask one question: is the noise about the work?

Old discipline

Quiet, still, seated, doing what you're told.

Refract discipline

Caring for your work, other people's work, the materials and the space.

FineTalking about the workTesting out loudLaughing at a surpriseExplaining to a friendMoving to see an idea

Check on itThe same few voices for agesOff-topic for a whileSomeone is upsetTools used unsafely

Movement is part of thinking. Movement carries ideas around the room. A learner who's moving is often a learner who's thinking.

Room agreements, including not wasting

AgreementThe reason we give
Take only what you need.So there's enough for everyone.
Use the offcuts box first.Most things can be made from what's already cut.
Ask before cutting into something new.Some things are hard to replace.
Put tools back where they live.So the next person can find them.
Ask before touching someone's work.Their work matters as much as yours.
Safety rules aren't up for choice.Everything else is.

Give the reason with every rule. Rules with reasons don't dampen engagement. The same rules given in a controlling way do.

6One approach, many formats

Taking things apart is one way in. Any format works if the learner can choose and find out.

Example: "What is sound?" in four stations

Learners visit at least two stations and pick one to go deep. Each station has a question card, not instructions.

Feel it

Change it

Rubber bands on a box, bottles of water.

Send it

See it

A sound-wave app, a tuning fork in water.

C

In the session

What the adult does while it happens.

7The facilitator

You're not the centre of the room any more. You're its most careful observer.

Where to look

The whole room

Every few minutes: who's absorbed, stuck, drifting?

Feelings first

A frustrated learner can't hear a hint.

The quiet ones

Silence can be focus or giving up. Go and look.

Signs of learning

Own goals, testing not guessing, explaining why, helping others.

Yourself

The urge to explain or rescue is your signal to wait.

With your body

  1. Get down to their level.
  2. Hands behind your back, off their work.
  3. Ask, then wait ten seconds.
  4. Keep a notebook for what you notice.
  5. Keep moving around the room.

With your words

SayInstead of
"What have you noticed?""Let me show you."
"What have you tried?""No, that's wrong."
"You might try…""You need to…"
"What makes you say that?""Good job!"
"Being stuck is part of it.""It's easy, look."
"What do you want to find out next?""Are you done?"

8When it gets hard

Frustration and distraction are part of it. Give the smallest help that works.

Frustration

Sighs, pushing it away, "this is stupid".

Try: Name it, then make the next step smaller.

Avoid: Fixing it for them.

Distraction

Wandering, off-topic chat, the phone.

Try: Ask "what are you working on?" Offer a challenge card or a break.

Avoid: Calling them out in front of everyone.

Can't choose

Hovering, "I don't know".

Try: Narrow it to two, or let them watch a friend first.

Avoid: Choosing for them straight away.

"I'm done" in five minutes

Finished and looking for the next thing.

Try: "Show me. What would make it harder?"

Avoid: Filling time with an unrelated task.

Wants the answer

Asking you, a friend or AI to just tell them.

Try: "What have you tried?" Then a hint, not the answer.

Avoid: Handing it over, or banning asking.

Takes over the group

One voice, one pair of hands.

Try: Give roles. Ask the quieter partner first.

Avoid: Telling them off in public.

Gives up quietly

Sitting still, hiding their work.

Try: Sit beside them. Make the next step tiny and winnable.

Avoid: Empty praise, or leaving them to it.

Mistakes are ladders

Each mistake is a rung, not a slip to fix. Learners learn this from how you react far more than from anything you tell them about it. That is how a growth mindset gets built.

Say: “Not yet.” “What did that tell you?” “Show me what you tried.”

Instead of: “Don't worry, it's easy.” “You're so smart.”

The hint ladder

Start at the bottom. Climb only as far as you need to get them moving, then step back.

9How we want them to feel

A journey map for one session. The dip in the middle is expected. Our job is to hold it.

A design picture of how we hope a session feels, not measured data.

Learner

Unsure, a bit curious → Welcome

Facilitator

2Notice and choose

Facilitator

3Plan a way in

Learner

Facilitator

4Try and find out

Learner

Absorbed → Absorbed

Facilitator

5Get stuck

Learner

Frustrated → "This is hard, and that's OK"

Facilitator

6Check in on yourself

Learner

Steadier → Aware of their progress

"I've already found three things" rebuilds confidence.

Facilitator

Feels: Relieved

Ask "how's it going?" before "what's next?"

Learner

Proud, sometimes shy → "I did that"

Facilitator

Feels: Rewarded

Teach from their attempts, not a script.

Learner

Facilitator

The science

The ten ideas underneath. Every "why it works" tag above links here.

Strong evidence Many studies or large trials agree.

Framework Built with educators. Useful for design, not tested as a cause.

Classic theory Widely cited. We haven't checked the original papers ourselves yet.

Mixed evidence Real but small effects that depend a lot on the setting.

Productive struggle

Strong evidence

Trying before being taught works when the struggle is supported. Unsupported struggle teaches less. Smaller benefit for younger children.

Shows up in: Eight things that make it work, When it gets hard, Get unstuck

Alfieri et al. 2011 · Sinha & Kapur 2021 · Beghetto 2017

Autonomy with structure

Strong evidence

Real choice inside a clear frame. The opposite of structure is chaos, not freedom.

Shows up in: Two ways to run a room, The driver's seat, What choosing does, Eight things that make it work, Noise and discipline, The facilitator, Find your reason and keep going

Jang, Reeve & Deci 2010 · Reeve & Cheon 2021

Cognitive load

Strong evidence

A mind holds only a few new things at once. Take away clutter, not challenge.

Shows up in: What choosing does, Lighten the load

Sweller, Kirschner & Clark 2007 · Sinha & Kapur 2021 · Cowan 2001 (cited, not yet checked by us)

Self-regulated learning

Strong evidence

Planning, checking and reviewing your own learning can be taught, and it pays off.

Shows up in: The facilitator, How we want them to feel, Get unstuck, Make it stick

EEF metacognition report 2020

Hard on purpose

Classic theory

Recalling instead of rereading, and spacing practice out, make learning last.

Shows up in: Make it stick

Roediger & Karpicke 2006; Dunlosky et al. 2013 (cited, not yet checked by us)

Information gap

Classic theory

Curiosity is the itch of a gap in what you know. A summary closes it too soon.

Shows up in: Many formats, Spark curiosity

Agency by Design, Project Zero · Loewenstein 1994; Gruber et al. 2014 (cited, not yet checked by us)

Distributed cognition

Framework

Thinking happens across people, tools and the room. A well-set room does part of it.

Shows up in: Eight things that make it work, Noise and discipline, Lighten the load, Spark curiosity

Agency by Design, Project Zero · Project Zero thinking routines · Exploratorium Learning Dimensions 2017 · Hutchins 1995 (cited, not yet checked by us)

Self-efficacy

Classic theory

"I can" grows from small real wins, and from watching someone like you.

Shows up in: How we want them to feel, Find your reason and keep going

Exploratorium Learning Dimensions 2017 · Bandura 1977 (cited, not yet checked by us)

Growth mindset

Mixed evidence

Believing you can get better with effort helps a little on its own. It helps most when the adults around a learner treat mistakes as steps, not failures. Children pick up how adults react to failure more than what adults say about ability.

Shows up in: When it gets hard, Get unstuck

Yeager et al. 2019 · Sisk et al. 2018 · Haimovitz & Dweck 2016 · Dweck 2006 (cited, not yet checked by us)

Emotion and learning

Classic theory

Fear and shame shut learning down. Interest and safety open it up.

Shows up in: Eight things that make it work, When it gets hard, How we want them to feel, Find your reason and keep going

Reeve & Cheon 2021 · Immordino-Yang & Damasio 2007 (cited, not yet checked by us)
