That autopilot is . It's why you can drive and plan a holiday at the same time, but you can't write an essay and plan a holiday. Writing still needs the desk. Most expertise is a partnership: the library handles the routine parts so the desk is free for the hard, new parts.

There's a catch. Because so much of an expert's skill runs on autopilot, they can't see it to explain it. Studies suggest experts can put only about 30% of what they do into words. This is the . Most people get noticeably faster within a few rounds. Each attempt moves a little of the skill from the desk into the library.

Researchers at Carnegie Mellon tracked thousands of learners in well-designed courses. People started at very different levels, but they improved at nearly the same rate with each practice attempt. That's the . "Slow learners" mostly needed more attempts, and kept going.

If you teach

A learner who starts low usually needs more practice opportunities, not a lower ceiling. Plan for extra attempts, and make sure each one comes with feedback.

Chapter 2

Wanting and stalling

Why the spark from a reel fades in a week, and what actually keeps people going.

The night Meera saved the reel, she felt a rush. A week later, the same reel barely moved her.

Kabir never felt a rush about chemistry at all. He knows it matters. That doesn't seem to help.

That rush was . It's often called the pleasure chemical, but it's mostly about wanting: it fires when a reward seems close. A finished ceiling that's months away barely registers, which is why matter so much.

Researchers define motivation as three behaviours: starting, keeping going, and putting in effort. Enjoying it isn't on the list. Musicians don't all love scales. They do them because of what scales get them.

    Chapter 4

    Hard on purpose

    Why the study habits that feel best often work worst, and what to do instead.

    Kabir finally sits down with chemistry. He rereads the chapter twice and highlights it in four colours. It all looks familiar. He feels ready.

    In the test, half of it is gone.

    Familiar isn't the same as remembered. Rereading makes things feel easy, and your brain mistakes that ease for learning. Researchers call this the .

    Try it

    A tiny experiment on yourself

    You'll learn eight made-up words. Four you'll reread three times. Four you'll see once, then quiz yourself on. Then a test.

    In the lab, the gap grows over days. In one classic study, students who tested themselves remembered far more a week later than students who reread, even though the rereaders felt more confident (Roediger and Karpicke, 2006). This is the .

    The good kind of hard

    Four habits that feel harder and work better

    Robert Bjork calls these . A big 2013 review rated them the most useful study techniques, and rated rereading and highlighting among the least (Dunlosky and colleagues).

    • Test yourself. Close the book and try to recall it. Flashcards, practice questions, explaining it to a friend.
    • Space it out. Three 20-minute sessions on different days beat one hour in one go. This is .
    • Mix it up. Practise different types of problems together rather than twenty of the same. This is .
    • Ask yourself why. Explain each step in your own words, as if teaching someone.
    Which one will you try for your goal?

    For a making project like Meera's, "testing yourself" means trying the step without the tutorial open, then checking.

    If you teach

    Start lessons with a quick no-stakes quiz on last week's material. It feels like assessment, but it's one of the cheapest ways to make learning stick.

    • Feeling familiar with something is not the same as remembering it.
    • Testing, spacing, mixing and explaining feel harder and work better.
